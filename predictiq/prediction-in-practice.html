Case 01 / Streaming and personalization

Netflix: from broad discovery to individualized recommendations

Documented company system · Historical research evidence

The business problem is discovery: a large catalog is valuable only if customers can find something they want to watch. The 2015 Netflix research paper describes how personalization supports recommendations, search and the presentation of titles. The "before" panel is a simplified comparison with generic discovery methods, not a claim that Netflix used only those methods before a specific launch date.

Before: broad or reactive discovery

  • Customers search by title or browse broad genres and popular-title lists.
  • The same highly popular titles can dominate what different viewers see.
  • A viewer may spend longer searching and may not discover relevant niche content.
  • Business decisions rely more on overall popularity and aggregate viewing patterns.

With predictive personalization

  • Rank titles and rows for each viewer using viewing and interaction signals.
  • Surface relevant genres, similar titles and contextual recommendations.
  • Adapt the interface as new interactions become available.
  • Test ranking and interface changes with online experiments rather than relying on intuition.

How prediction works, step by step

01Collect

Viewing activity, interactions, searches and title metadata.

02Prepare

Create user and title features; account for recency and preferences.

03Predict

Estimate relevance and rank candidate titles using multiple algorithms.

04Present

Show personalized rows, titles and discovery experiences.

05Evaluate

A/B-test discovery, engagement and retention-related outcomes.

One customer journey — illustrative

  • Viewer A watches several science-fiction series and frequently completes documentaries.
  • Generic experience: the viewer sees a broad popular-title list, including many unrelated programs.
  • Personalized experience: relevant science-fiction and documentary titles are ranked higher.
  • Viewer A starts a recommended title; the interaction becomes another signal for future recommendations.

What changes for the customer and the business

  • Customer: less effort to discover relevant titles and more personalized browsing.
  • Business: better content discovery and opportunities to improve engagement and retention.
  • Product team: online experiments can evaluate changes instead of relying solely on opinions.
  • Caution: excessive personalization can reduce diversity and create feedback loops.

Case 02 / Fashion retail and demand

Fashion retail: from aggregate buying to preference-informed inventory

Research-based proposed approach · Not verified retailer deployment

Your presentation discusses research using fitting-room preference information, clustering and decision trees to improve fashion product matching. The process below shows how such a retailer could operate, rather than claiming a specific company implemented it and achieved measured results.

Before: conventional planning

  • Buyers estimate demand from last season's sales and staff experience.
  • Stock decisions emphasize aggregate category totals and past bestsellers.
  • Limited insight into why shoppers try products but do not buy.
  • Wrong sizes or styles can lead to stockouts, markdowns or excess inventory.

With predictive preference analysis

  • Combine permitted try-on preferences, sales, returns and product attributes.
  • Cluster customers by style, fit and purchasing behavior.
  • Use decision trees to identify patterns associated with purchase outcomes.
  • Forecast category and size-level demand to guide buying and replenishment.

How the retailer could make predictions

01Collect

Record sales, returns, fit feedback and fitting-room preferences where permitted.

02Prepare

Standardize sizes and styles; remove duplicates; handle missing data.

03Segment

Use K-means to explore groups with similar preferences.

04Predict

Use labeled models for purchase likelihood and time series for demand.

05Act & learn

Adjust recommendations and stock; measure returns, sales and stockouts.

Illustrative shopper example

  • Customer B frequently tries neutral-colored running shoes in size 39 but buys only cushioned models.
  • The retailer sees repeated fit preferences, not merely a generic footwear interest.
  • An online or in-store recommendation prioritizes available cushioned shoes in the correct size.
  • Merchandising reviews whether demand for that style and size is underrepresented in past sales because of stockouts.

How the approach could benefit operations

  • Customer: better size and style relevance, less time searching, fewer unsuitable suggestions.
  • Retailer: improved product matching and potential reduction in markdowns and returns.
  • Supply chain: better replenishment planning by category, store and size.
  • Caution: fitting-room data can be incomplete or sensitive in context; validate collection, consent where required and fairness.

Illustrative footwear demand comparison

This is the hypothetical numerical example already used in your MBA material. It illustrates a possible inventory benefit, not a measured result from the academic study.

Metric Conventional order Predictive-assisted plan
Pairs ordered 1,000 850
Actual demand 880 880
Unsold units if no replenishment 120 0
Potential unmet demand 0 30
Operational trade-off Higher excess stock Lower excess stock, but stockout risk

The predictive-assisted order is closer to actual demand (absolute error 30 vs 120), but ordering 850 against demand of 880 creates a potential 30-unit shortage. A responsible decision also considers lost sales, safety stock, lead time, and replenishment speed.

Business lesson: Predictive accuracy is not identical to business success. The best inventory decision balances carrying costs, stockout costs, customer satisfaction and uncertainty.

MBA synthesis

How prediction changes customer strategy

Dimension Netflix Fashion retail scenario
Old decision Broad discovery and popularity Historical aggregate buying
New signal Individual viewing and interaction behavior Fit, style, sales, returns and preferences
Prediction Which title is relevant to this viewer? Which item suits this shopper? How much stock is needed?
Customer change Personalized content discovery More relevant product matching and availability
Business benefit to test Engagement and retention Conversion, returns and inventory efficiency
Evidence status Documented historical company research Research-inspired hypothetical implementation
Conclusion: The shift is from reacting to what customers already did toward estimating what they may want next. A trustworthy system measures whether those predictions actually improve customer experience and incremental business value.
